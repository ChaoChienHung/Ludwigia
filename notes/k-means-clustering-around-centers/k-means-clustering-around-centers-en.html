As we explored in Discovering Hidden Structures: What Clustering Really DoesNotesDiscovering Hidden Structures: What Clustering Really DoesData MiningClusteringA concept-first overview of clustering as a way to reveal structure in unlabeled data through representation, similarity, data quality, and structural assumptions., clustering is fundamentally about grouping objects together based on a chosen perspective of similarity. Depending on their core mechanisms, clustering algorithms generally fall into three main categories: centroid-based, density-based, and hierarchical clustering.

In this note, we will explore K-Means, one of the most classic centroid-based algorithms. As its category suggests, K-Means uses a central point (a centroid) to represent an entire cluster, and assigns data points to these clusters based on their distance to these representatives. Consequently, a good clustering is one that minimizes the total distance between data points and their assigned representatives, resulting in compact clusters whose members lie closely around their centers.

However, this simple premise introduces a core dilemma: calculating a cluster's centroid (its arithmetic mean) requires knowing which data points belong to that cluster in the first place. But in unlabeled data, cluster memberships are precisely the unknown information we are trying to discover! How can we find optimal centroids when they depend on assignments we don't yet have? Beyond this fundamental chicken-and-egg problem, what practical details must we pay attention to when implementing the algorithm? When is K-Means the right tool for the job, and when is it destined to fail? Finally, are there related variants we can turn to for different outcomes?

In the following sections, we will delve into these questions one by one.

1. Representing a Cluster: The Birth of the Centroid

In many practical applications, we don't just want to partition data; we want to summarize it. If we are clustering customer behaviors, we want a single "typical customer profile" to represent a group. If we are grouping delivery locations, we want a single coordinate to act as the regional hub.

Therefore, our initial goal is simple: we want to find a single point, let's call it $c$, to act as the representative for an entire cluster $C$.

Step 1: Evaluating the Representative

If $c$ is going to be a good representative, the data points in the cluster should lie as close to it as possible. We can quantify exactly how "good" a specific point $c$ is by measuring the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE). We define the cost function $J(c)$ as the total squared distance from every point $x$ in the cluster to this representative $c$:

$$ J(c) = \sum_{x \in C} \lVert x - c \rVert^2 $$

Notice that at this stage, $c$ could be any point in space. Our goal is to find the absolute best $c$ that minimizes this cost.

Step 2: Finding the Optimal Point

To find the optimal representative that minimizes the squared distances, we can take the partial derivative of $J$ with respect to $c$ and set it to zero:

$$ \frac{\partial J}{\partial c} = -2\sum_{x \in C}(x - c) = 0 $$

By expanding this equation, we get:

$$ \sum_{x \in C} x - \sum_{x \in C} c = 0 $$

Since summing the constant $c$ over all $|C|$ points in the cluster is simply $|C|c$, we have:

$$ |C|c = \sum_{x \in C} x $$

Dividing both sides by the number of points $|C|$, we arrive at the perfect representative point:

$$ c = \frac{1}{|C|} \sum_{x \in C} x $$

We define this optimal, SSE-minimizing point as the centroid (denoted as $\mu$). This is a profound realization: K-Means does not just arbitrarily decide to use the "average." It uses the mean because calculus proves it is the exact mathematical minimizer of the squared distance.

Step 3: The Pairwise Bonus (Centroid Identity)

At this point, a skeptic might ask a very valid question: "By summarizing the cluster with a single point, aren't we ignoring the true essence of clustering? Shouldn't clustering be about ensuring that data points are close to EACH OTHER, not just close to some artificial center?"

Intuitively, to verify that a cluster is truly tight, we would want to measure the pairwise distances between every single point in the cluster. But computing point-to-point distances for $N$ points requires $O(N^2)$ operations, which becomes a computational nightmare for large datasets.

Does optimizing for our representative centroid $\mu$ actually achieve the goal of bringing points closer to each other?

The answer is a resounding yes, thanks to a beautiful mathematical result known as the Centroid Identity (also called Huygens' Theorem). It states that the sum of squared distances from the points to their centroid is exactly proportional to the total pairwise squared distance within that cluster:

$$ \sum_{x \in C} \lVert x - \mu \rVert^2 = \frac{1}{2|C|} \sum_{x \in C} \sum_{y \in C} \lVert x - y \rVert^2 $$

Let's briefly see why this identity holds. Let the cluster contain $n = |C|$ points, denoted by $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$, and let $\mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i$.

First, expand the left-hand side:

$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert x_i-\mu\rVert^2 &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( \lVert x_i\rVert^2 -2x_i^\top\mu +\lVert\mu\rVert^2 \right) \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert x_i\rVert^2 - 2\mu^\top\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i + n\lVert\mu\rVert^2 \end{aligned} $$

Since $\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i=n\mu$, we obtain:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert x_i-\mu\rVert^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert x_i\rVert^2 - n\lVert\mu\rVert^2 $$

Now expand the pairwise distances on the right side:

$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \lVert x_i-x_j\rVert^2 &= \sum_{i,j} \left( \lVert x_i\rVert^2 + \lVert x_j\rVert^2 - 2x_i^\top x_j \right) \\ &= 2n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert x_i\rVert^2 - 2\left\| \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i \right\|^2 \\ &= 2n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert x_i\rVert^2 - 2n^2\lVert\mu\rVert^2 \end{aligned} $$

Dividing both sides by $2n$ gives:

$$ \frac{1}{2n} \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \lVert x_i-x_j\rVert^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert x_i\rVert^2 - n\lVert\mu\rVert^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert x_i-\mu\rVert^2 $$

Which is exactly the Centroid Identity.

What does this mean for K-Means? Because the scaling factor $\frac{1}{2|C|}$ depends only on the cluster size, minimizing one quantity is exactly equivalent to minimizing the other. In other words, bringing every point closer to the centroid is mathematically the exact same thing as bringing every point closer to every other point.

We get the best of both worlds: we successfully summarize the cluster using a single representative point, we achieve the true clustering goal of minimizing pairwise distances, and we drastically reduce the computational cost from $O(N^2)$ down to $O(N)$.

The Euclidean Catch (and a Clever Workaround)

It is crucial to note, however, that this elegant equivalence comes with a strict condition: it only holds true in Euclidean space. This is a fundamental limitation of K-Means—it is strictly bound to using Euclidean distance (or geometric distance) as its similarity metric. You cannot simply swap in other distance metrics (like Manhattan or raw Cosine distance) and expect the same mathematical derivation to work.

Fortunately, there is a very practical workaround, especially for modern machine learning embeddings. If you apply L2 normalization to your data (turning them into unit vectors), the squared Euclidean distance becomes inversely proportional to Cosine similarity. This means that while vanilla K-Means doesn't support Cosine similarity out of the box, you can perfectly cluster text or image embeddings based on their angles just by normalizing the vectors first!

2. The Optimization Process: Global SSE and Lloyd's Algorithm

In Section 1, we proved that if we have a specific group of points, the centroid (the arithmetic mean) is the absolute best representative for them.

But a clustering algorithm doesn't just evaluate one cluster—it evaluates the entire dataset partitioned into $K$ clusters. The global objective of K-Means is to minimize the total Sum of Squared Errors (SSE) across all $K$ clusters simultaneously:

$$ \mathrm{Global\ SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{K} \sum_{x \in C_i} \lVert x - \mu_i \rVert^2 $$

Solving the Chicken-and-Egg Problem

To minimize this Global SSE, we are faced with a circular dependency:

  • To find the best centers ($\mu_i$), you need to know which points belong to which clusters ($C_i$).
  • To assign points to the best clusters ($C_i$), you need to know where the centers ($\mu_i$) are.

K-Means solves this using Lloyd's algorithm, which breaks the problem down by repeatedly fixing one half of the equation while solving the other:

  1. Initialize: Randomly place $K$ centroids in the space.
  2. Assign (Fix Centers): Assuming the centers are perfectly placed, assign each point to its nearest centroid. Because points join the cluster that offers the smallest squared distance, this step mathematically minimizes the overall SSE.
  3. Update (Fix Assignments): Assuming the cluster assignments are perfect, recompute each centroid as the mean of its newly assigned points. As we proved in Section 1, the mean is the exact minimizer of the SSE for a fixed set of points.
  4. Repeat: Alternate between Step 2 and Step 3 until the assignments stop changing.

This alternating structure is the core reason K-Means is so effective. Because every single step mathematically reduces (or maintains) the Global SSE, the algorithm is strictly monotonic and is guaranteed to converge. It elegantly breaks an infinitely complex optimization problem into two simple, greedy steps.

3. Implementation Details: Initialization and Choosing $K$

While K-Means is mathematically elegant, Lloyd's algorithm has a major catch: it guarantees convergence, but only to a local minimum, not necessarily the global optimum. This makes practical implementation details crucial.

The Initialization Problem If you start with bad initial centroids (e.g., two centroids placed right next to each other in the same natural group), the algorithm can easily get permanently stuck in a sub-optimal arrangement. Different random seeds will yield noticeably different clusterings.

This is why practitioners usually run K-Means multiple times and keep the result with the lowest final SSE. To systematically fix this, K-Means++ was introduced. It uses a smarter initialization strategy that probabilistically spreads the initial centers as far apart as possible, drastically reducing the chance of poor convergence.

The $K$ Problem The optimization math is clean only after $K$ is fixed. But in reality, $K$ is rarely known in advance.

  • If $K$ is too small, distinct natural groups get merged.
  • If $K$ is too large, natural groups get artificially fragmented.
  • You cannot just pick the $K$ with the lowest SSE, because SSE will always decrease as $K$ increases (reaching zero if every point becomes its own cluster).

Choosing $K$ remains an awkward mix of domain knowledge, heuristics (like the Elbow Method), and downstream validation. K-Means is not a fully automatic pattern detector; it requires human guidance to set the boundaries.

4. Pros, Cons, and Geometric Assumptions

To understand when K-Means works and when it fails, you just need to look at what its objective function rewards. By minimizing squared Euclidean distance to a single center, K-Means is inherently biased toward a specific geometry.

Where K-Means Shines: K-Means is incredibly fast, simple, and interpretable. It works exceptionally well when your data is:

  • Pockets of compact, roughly spherical groups.
  • Of somewhat similar scale and density.
  • Separated enough that nearest-center assignments make logical sense.

In applications like basic customer segmentation, vector quantization, or image color compression, K-Means provides a clean summary. The resulting centroids are highly interpretable—they literally serve as readable "prototypes" for the group they represent.

Where K-Means Breaks: K-Means does not fail randomly; it fails predictably when data violates its geometric worldview. Common failure modes include:

  • Irregular or non-convex shapes: If clusters are crescent-shaped or form concentric rings, a single centroid is a terrible summary.
  • Different scales and densities: If one cluster is tiny and dense while another is broad and sparse, the squared-error objective may incorrectly chop the large cluster in half.
  • Outliers: Because distances are squared, extreme outliers act like gravity wells, pulling the mean—and therefore the centroid—far away from the actual data mass.

If your data relies on connectivity (like a chain of points) rather than central tendency, K-Means will still force the data into rigid, spherical boxes anyway.

5. The K-Means Family: Beyond the Vanilla Algorithm

Once the core K-Means idea is clear, it is easier to see why a whole family of variants keeps emerging. They all share the same center-based logic, but extend it to patch specific weaknesses:

  • K-Medoids: Keeps the center-based view but restricts the center to be an actual data point rather than a computed mean. This makes it far more robust to extreme outliers and easier to interpret in non-continuous spaces.
  • X-Means: Attempts to automate the painful process of choosing $K$ by allowing the model to adaptively split clusters based on statistical criteria.
  • RK-Means (Robust K-Means): Modifies the objective function or weighting scheme to prevent noise and outliers from ruining the centroid updates.
  • Mini-Batch K-Means: An algorithmic tweak that processes small random subsets of data at a time, drastically speeding up convergence for massive, web-scale datasets.

Summary

K-Means is powerful because it translates our visual intuition of "tight groups" into a concrete optimization problem: minimizing the within-cluster squared distance to representative centers.

That single objective explains everything about the algorithm. It explains why we use means, why Lloyd's algorithm ping-pongs between fixing assignments and fixing centers, why bad initialization leads to bad local minima, and why the algorithm stubbornly assumes every cluster in the world is a neat, compact sphere. If your data fits that assumption, K-Means is hard to beat. If it doesn't, understanding why K-Means failed is often your best clue for what to try next.

Key Takeaways

  • K-Means avoids the $O(N^2)$ pairwise distance trap by using centroids, leveraging the mathematical proof that minimizing distance to the mean implicitly minimizes all pairwise distances.
  • The algorithm's ultimate goal is to minimize the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE).
  • The arithmetic mean is naturally chosen because it is the exact mathematical minimizer for squared Euclidean distance.
  • Lloyd's algorithm alternates between assigning points to the nearest center and updating centers to the mean of their points, guaranteeing convergence to a local minimum.
  • Because it optimizes squared distance to a center, K-Means strongly assumes clusters are spherical, similarly sized, and free of massive outliers.
  • Variants like K-Medoids (for robustness) and K-Means++ (for better initialization) patch the algorithm's practical flaws without abandoning its center-based philosophy.
